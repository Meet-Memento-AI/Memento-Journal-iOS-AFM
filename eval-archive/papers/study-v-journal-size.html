Conversation Simulation · Study V

How big does a journal have to be?

Four studies compared an empty journal with a 262-entry one. Every real user starts between them. This sweeps all one hundred sizes in the gap — and finds that the retriever does not care about size at all, which turns out to be both the design working and the thing that hides the harm.

Spearman rank correlation between journal size and citation rate, across 96 sizes from five entries to a hundred. On the turns where a citation is the right answer it is +0.003. Corpus size does not move the rate.

01 · The mechanismAn entry has to stand out from its own corpus

EntryRetriever.semanticThreshold is relative, not absolute. Above a five-entry corpus it returns max(floor, μ + 1.0σ) over the cosine similarities of the corpus being searched; below five it returns a flat floor + 0.05 = 0.35, because μ and σ over four points mean nothing.

The consequence was pre-registered before the run, because it follows from the arithmetic rather than the data: under any roughly bell-shaped similarity distribution, a fixed share of entries sits above one standard deviation however many there are. So the retriever should fire at a near-constant rate whether the journal holds five entries or a hundred.

It does. That is prediction P2, and it held more tightly than the threshold asked for.

02 · What the flat rate hidesSplit by what was actually asked

A citation rate over every turn mixes in the turn types where a citation would be wrong. share, social and offdomain turns cite 0% at every single size, by design — they are someone talking, not someone asking. They dilute everything.

Restricted to journalQuery — a person asking the app about their own journal — the picture is not flat at all.

The product answer

Citation on journal questions climbs 46.2% (≤4 entries) → 71.4% (5–7) → 80.0% (11–15), then sits in the seventies for the remaining eighty-five sizes. The journal becomes useful at about ten entries. Onboarding that reaches ten is buying the feature the app is named for.

03 · The harmA nearly-empty journal is not quiet — it is wrong

The obvious worry about a small journal is that the app says nothing useful. The measurement says the opposite. Below five entries the app keeps answering, and hall.uncitedQuote — a quoted span that nothing in the journal actually contains — fires at more than four times its rate everywhere else.

04 · ScorecardSeven predictions, committed before generation

The pre-registration was written and committed before a single message was generated. Four held, three were falsified — and two of those three are facts about the predictions rather than about the product.

05 · My own errorsTwo thresholds anchored to the wrong baseline

P1 predicted a ≥10pp citation gap below five entries and measured +8.1pp, so it fails. But the same comparison on the turns where a citation is the right answer is +28.1pp — 46.2% against 74.3%. The cliff is real and larger than predicted; I anchored the threshold to a denominator diluted by turn types that never cite.

P4 predicted gating ≤6% in every bin, citing the empty arm's 5.5%. That was the wrong anchor and I knew it before the results landed: no seeded arm in four studies has ever gated below 11%. An empty journal gates low because nothing retrieves and most turns are social. P4 duly failed at 17.5% and says nothing whatever about corpus size. It was recorded as mis-anchored in the repository before the run finished, rather than reinterpreted afterwards.

Why this is in the paper A pre-registration you are allowed to quietly reinterpret afterwards is not a pre-registration. Two of three falsifications being my own aim rather than the product's behaviour is the system working: the errors are visible and dated, which is the only property that makes the four surviving predictions worth anything.

06 · P3The pack really is starved, and it changes almost nothing

P3 predicted that phantom markers — {{quote:4}} pointing at a slot that does not exist — would at least double below five entries, because maxEntries = 5 means a four-entry journal cannot fill the evidence pack. The premise was correct and measurable.

The consequence did not follow. Phantom markers run at 0.198 per turn below five entries and 0.160 above twenty — a 1.24× difference against a predicted 2×. They are, to a good approximation, size-independent at about 0.16 per turn.

That matters beyond this study. Studies III and IV concluded that the app's reference failures come from the contract being imperfectly followed, not from the model being too small or the evidence too thin. Study V had a clean opportunity to attribute marker failure to corpus size — one-slot packs against five-slot packs, same model, same prompt — and found it cannot. The model misuses markers at the same rate whether it has one slot or five.

07 · The strongest numberZero fabricated quotes, at every size

Study II's seeded arm invented quotations in 911 of 1,626 generated turns — 56.0%. Spec 050 replaced italics-as-quotation with typed markers the renderer resolves against an evidence pack, and Study III measured that at zero.

Study V extends the claim in the direction that could most plausibly have broken it: 0 fabricated quotes in 3,332 turns across all one hundred journal sizes, including the regime where the pack holds a single slot and the model has least to work with. The contract is not just effective, it is invariant to how much evidence exists.

Latency is the other clean result: p50 3.65s at ten entries or fewer against 3.98s at ninety or more — 1.09×. Retrieval cost is nothing against generation, so nothing here argues for capping journal size.

08 · What to changeA size-aware floor, not a prompt

The study points at one change, and it is small.

  1. Raise the sub-five-entry floor, or suppress quotation below roughly ten entries. The flat 0.35 is the one place the retriever already knows the corpus is too small to reason about statistically, and it is exactly where unbacked quotation concentrates. Then re-measure hall.uncitedQuote in the 1–4 band with this same harness.
  2. Extend the sweep past 100 with an identical question-draw rule on both sides, to settle whether 74% → 90.5% between 100 and 262 entries is size or question mix.
  3. Separate size from recency. Arms here are the most recent n entries, so a small journal also spans a short window. An arm of n entries sampled across the whole nine months, against the same n taken as a suffix, would tell these apart.

09 · ThreatsWhat this design cannot tell you

  • Size and recency are confounded by construction. Arms are the most recent n entries — a one-entry arm is a single day, a hundred-entry arm spans five months. Any size effect here could be a recency effect and this design cannot separate them. This is the study's main threat and it was named in the pre-registration, not discovered afterwards.
  • Two sessions per size. The sweep is wide, not deep. No per-size point is a result; every verdict is read off the pre-registered bins. The smallest bins are genuinely thin — 13 journalQuery turns below five entries.
  • No relevance labels. "Cited" is never "cited aptly". The harness can prove a quote is unbacked; it cannot prove a correctly backed citation was the right entry to reach for. This is the single biggest gap between what was measured and what a user cares about.
  • P7 passed on a thin denominator. A one-entry journal cited on 6/33 turns, inside the 20% line — but it drew only one journalQuery turn in two sessions. P7 should not be read as evidence that a one-entry journal behaves well; the 1–4 band's 46.2% citation and 7.9% unbacked quotation are the better guide, and they point the other way.
  • One authored persona, both interlocutors synthetic — as in every study in this series. A self-play harness measures the app against a model's idea of a person.
  • 100 is not a large journal. The fixture corpus caps at 262 entries and the sweep stops at 100 by design.
  • 42 turns ended in guardrailRefusal, which is specified behaviour, and 3 in an unavailable runtime fault, out of 3,395 assistant turns.
